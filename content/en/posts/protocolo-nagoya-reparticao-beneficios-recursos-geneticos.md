Brazil is the country with the greatest biodiversity on the planet, holding between 15% and 20% of all global biological diversity. This wealth, however, only generates value if it is accessed, studied, and explored under clear rules — which simultaneously ensure innovation and fair sharing of benefits with those who protect this heritage. That is the purpose of the Nagoya Protocol and, in Brazil, of Law 13.123/2015 (Biodiversity Law). This article explains what the Protocol is, why it matters for a megadiverse country, and how Brazilian legislation implements it in practice, from registration in SisGen to benefit-sharing.

## What is the Nagoya Protocol

The Nagoya Protocol on Access to Genetic Resources and the Fair and Equitable Sharing of Benefits Arising from their Utilization is an international agreement supplementary to the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), adopted on October 29, 2010, during the 10th Conference of the Parties, in Nagoya, Japan. It entered into force internationally on October 12, 2014, 90 days after the deposit of the 50th instrument of ratification, in accordance with the [Convention on Biological Diversity](https://www.cbd.int/abs/about/).

The Protocol's objective is to create a transparent legal framework for the fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the utilization of genetic resources, one of the three pillars of the CBD — alongside the conservation of biodiversity and the sustainable use of its components. In practice, it establishes the rules for a country that provides genetic resources (for example, a plant native to the Amazon or the Cerrado) to receive a portion of the gains obtained by those who develop and commercialize products from these resources.

Brazil signed the Protocol on February 2, 2011; the National Congress approved it through [Legislative Decree No. 136, of August 11, 2020](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2023-2026/2023/decreto/d11865.htm); the instrument of ratification was deposited on March 4, 2021; and the treaty entered into force for the country on June 2, 2021. Internal promulgation occurred only in December 2023, through [Decree No. 11,865, of December 27, 2023](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2023-2026/2023/decreto/d11865.htm).

## Why Nagoya matters for a megadiverse country

The importance of the Protocol is not uniform across countries: it weighs much more heavily on megadiverse nations. According to Brazil's profile on the [Convention on Biological Diversity](https://www.cbd.int/countries/profile/?country=br), the country leads the ranking among the 17 megadiverse countries in the world, is second in endemism (species exclusive to its territory), and hosts two biodiversity hotspots: the Atlantic Forest and the Cerrado. At least 103,870 animal species and 43,020 plant species are known in the country, and it is estimated that Brazil concentrates between 15% and 20% of global biodiversity.

It is precisely this position that makes the access regime decisive. Each sample of genetic heritage collected in Brazilian territory — a soil microorganism, a native plant variety, an extract from a traditionally used plant — carries genetic information that can be transformed into a product of high commercial value in the cosmetics, food, agricultural, and biotechnology industries. Without a framework regulating who accesses, under what conditions, and how benefits are shared, the country exports genetic raw material without return, and the communities that hold traditional knowledge about these species are left without protection and without a share in the gains.

Associated traditional knowledge is the second axis of the Protocol: practices, knowledge, and uses developed by indigenous peoples, traditional communities, and traditional farmers regarding biodiversity. The treaty recognizes these holders as legitimate partners in benefit-sharing, not merely as a source of free information.

## Law 13.123/2015: the Brazilian legal framework

Brazil did not wait for the promulgation of the Protocol to structure its legislation. [Law No. 13,123 of May 20, 2015](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2015/lei/l13123.htm) — the Biodiversity Law — regulates item II of §1 and §4 of Article 225 of the Federal Constitution and the provisions of the CBD on access to genetic heritage, protection of associated traditional knowledge, and benefit-sharing. It revoked the former Provisional Measure 2.186-16/2001 and entered into force 180 days after its publication.

The law defines the central concepts of the regime: genetic heritage (information of genetic origin from plant, animal, microbial, or other species, including derived substances), associated traditional knowledge (information or practice of an indigenous population, traditional community, or traditional farmer associated with genetic heritage), finished product, and reproductive material. It also creates the Genetic Heritage Management Council (CGen), a deliberative, normative, advisory, and appellate collegiate body within the Ministry of the Environment, responsible for coordinating access and benefit-sharing policy.

The law's design separates two stages:

- **Scientific research:** access to genetic heritage for research is simplified — a declaratory electronic registration is sufficient, with no need for prior authorization. The goal is not to hinder science.
- **Economic exploitation:** when access results in a finished product or commercialized reproductive material, the obligation to share benefits arises.

This separation is the heart of the regime and what made Law 13.123 more agile than the previous regime, which required prior authorization for practically everything and generated years of legal uncertainty in Brazilian research.

## Benefit-sharing: the rules of articles 19 to 26

Benefit-sharing may be monetary or non-monetary (art. 19). Non-monetary modalities include biodiversity conservation projects at the species' occurrence site, technology transfer, making products available in the public domain, free-of-charge licensing, and training of human resources.

When the modality is monetary and the finished product or reproductive material results from access to genetic heritage, the user must pay **1% of the annual net revenue** obtained from economic exploitation (art. 20). The amount may be reduced to as low as 0.1% through a sectoral agreement negotiated with the Union (art. 21). In non-monetary modalities, the sharing must be equivalent to 75% of the monetary value, according to CGen criteria (art. 22).

The treatment of associated traditional knowledge distinguishes two situations:

- **Unidentifiable origin:** when it is not possible to identify the population holding the knowledge, benefit-sharing is mandatorily monetary, at the same percentages (arts. 23 and 25). In this case, the benefit-sharing agreement is entered into between the user and the Union, represented by the Ministry of the Environment.
- **Identifiable origin:** when the provider of the knowledge is identifiable, benefit-sharing is negotiated directly between the user and the provider, in a fair and equitable manner, with clauses on conditions, obligations, types, and duration of benefits (art. 24). The existence of other holders of the same knowledge is absolutely presumed, and they receive their share through the National Fund for Benefit-Sharing (FNRB).

Benefit-sharing agreements must indicate the parties, the products exploited, the term, the modality, the rights and responsibilities, the intellectual property clause, the penalties, and the venue in Brazil (art. 26). Non-compliance subjects the user to administrative sanctions ranging from a warning to fines, seizure of samples, and suspension of the product's manufacture and sale (art. 27).

## SisGen: the registration system in practice

Electronic access registration is done in SisGen — the National System for the Management of Genetic Heritage and Associated Traditional Knowledge. SisGen was created by [Decree No. 8,772 of May 11, 2016](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2016/decreto/d8772.htm), which regulates Law 13.123/2015, and is operated by the Executive Secretariat of CGen.

In SisGen, the user declares activities of access to genetic heritage and associated traditional knowledge, the sending of samples for services abroad, the shipment of samples, and the economic exploitation of a finished product or reproductive material. The system also manages benefit-sharing agreements and the payment of installments due.

For companies and researchers, the practical rule is: **all access to Brazilian genetic heritage must be registered in SisGen**, and the economic exploitation of a derived product requires an up-to-date benefit-sharing agreement. Registration is declaratory — the user is responsible for the truthfulness of the information — and the system began operating fully in November 2017, when the deadline for regularizing activities initiated before the law ended.

## What to watch going forward

The late promulgation of the Nagoya Protocol (Decree 11,865/2023) ends a cycle of legal uncertainty: the treaty now has the force of domestic law, and Brazil is formally aligned with the international regime for access and benefit-sharing. The points of attention for companies and research institutions are:

- **Continuous compliance:** register every new access in SisGen and keep economic exploitation information up to date. The fine for unregistered access and for lack of benefit-sharing can reach R$ 10 million for legal entities (art. 27, §5).
- **Sectoral agreements:** sectors that deem the 1% of net revenue disproportionate may seek sectoral agreements to reduce the rate to 0.1% — an instrument still little used, but provided for by law.
- **Relationship with industrial property law:** benefit-sharing does not prevent patenting, but the intellectual property clause is an essential element of the agreement. For biotechnology, the regime adds to other international frameworks — such as the [Budapest Treaty]({{< relref "posts/brasil-tratado-budapeste-patentes-biotecnologia/" >}}), which Brazil joined for the international deposit of microorganisms.

For a country that holds the greatest biodiversity in the world, the biodiversity law is, at the same time, an instrument for protecting national heritage and providing legal certainty for innovation. The Nagoya Protocol and Law 13.123/2015 form the framework that allows Brazil to charge for what nature offers — and the productive sector to innovate without risk.
