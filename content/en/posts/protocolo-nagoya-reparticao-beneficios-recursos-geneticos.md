Brazil is the country with the greatest biodiversity on the planet and holds between 15% and 20% of all global biological diversity. That wealth, however, only generates value if it is accessed, studied, and explored under clear rules—ensuring both innovation and fair sharing of benefits with those who protect this heritage. That is the purpose of the Nagoya Protocol and, in Brazil, of Law 13.123/2015 (Biodiversity Law). This article explains what the Protocol is, why it matters for a megadiverse country, and how Brazilian law implements it in practice, from SisGen registration to benefit-sharing.

## What is the Nagoya Protocol

The Nagoya Protocol on Access to Genetic Resources and the Fair and Equitable Sharing of Benefits Arising from their Utilization is an international agreement supplementary to the Convention on Biological Diversity (CBD), adopted on October 29, 2010 during the 10th Conference of the Parties, in Nagoya, Japan. It entered into force internationally on October 12, 2014, 90 days after the deposit of the 50th instrument of ratification, according to the [Convention on Biological Diversity](https://www.cbd.int/abs/about/).

The Protocol aims to create a transparent legal framework for the fair and equitable sharing of benefits arising from the use of genetic resources, one of the three pillars of the CBD—alongside biodiversity conservation and the sustainable use of its components. In practice, it establishes the rules for a country that provides genetic resources (for example, a native plant from the Amazon or the Cerrado) to receive a share of the gains obtained by those who develop and sell products from these resources.

Brazil signed the Protocol on February 2, 2011; the National Congress approved it through [Legislative Decree No. 136 of August 11, 2020](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2023-2026/2023/decreto/d11865.htm); the instrument of ratification was deposited on March 4, 2021; and the treaty entered into force for the country on June 2, 2021. Domestic promulgation took place only in December 2023, through [Decree No. 11,865 of December 27, 2023](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2023-2026/2023/decreto/d11865.htm).

## Why Nagoya matters for a megadiverse country

The Protocol's importance is not uniform across countries: it weighs far more on megadiverse nations. According to Brazil's profile on the [Convention on Biological Diversity](https://www.cbd.int/countries/profile/?country=br), the country leads the ranking among the 17 megadiverse countries in the world, ranks second in endemism (species exclusive to its territory), and hosts two biodiversity hotspots: the Atlantic Forest and the Cerrado. At least 103,870 animal species and 43,020 plant species are known in the country, and Brazil is estimated to hold between 15% and 20% of global biodiversity.

It is precisely this position that makes the access regime decisive. Every sample of genetic heritage collected in Brazilian territory—a soil microorganism, a native plant variety, a traditionally used plant extract—carries genetic information that can become a high-commercial-value product in the cosmetics, food, agricultural, and biotechnology industries. Without a framework regulating who accesses, under what conditions, and how benefits are shared, the country exports genetic raw material without return, and the communities that hold traditional knowledge about these species are left unprotected and without a share of the gains.

Associated traditional knowledge is the Protocol's second axis: practices, knowledge, and uses of biodiversity developed by indigenous peoples, traditional communities, and traditional farmers. The treaty recognizes these holders as legitimate partners in benefit-sharing, not merely as a source of free information.

## Law 13.123/2015: the Brazilian legal framework

Brazil did not wait for the Protocol's promulgation to structure its legislation. [Law No. 13,123 of May 20, 2015](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2015/lei/l13123.htm)—the Biodiversity Law—regulates item II of § 1 and § 4 of Article 225 of the Federal Constitution and the provisions of the CBD on access to genetic heritage, protection of associated traditional knowledge, and benefit-sharing. It revoked the former Provisional Measure 2.186-16/2001 and entered into force 180 days after its publication.

The law defines the central concepts of the regime: genetic heritage (genetic information from plant, animal, microbial, or other nature, including derived substances), associated traditional knowledge (information or practice of an indigenous population, traditional community, or traditional farmer associated with genetic heritage), finished product, and reproductive material. It also creates the Genetic Heritage Management Council (CGen), a collegiate body with deliberative, regulatory, advisory, and appellate functions within the Ministry of the Environment, responsible for coordinating access and benefit-sharing policy.

The law's design separates two phases:

- **Scientific research:** access to genetic heritage for research is simplified—a declaratory electronic registration is enough, with no need for prior authorization. The goal is not to slow down science.
- **Economic exploitation:** when access results in a marketed finished product or reproductive material, the obligation to share benefits arises.

This separation is the heart of the regime and is what made Law 13.123 more agile than the previous regime, which required prior authorization for virtually everything and generated years of legal uncertainty in Brazilian research.

## Benefit-sharing: the rules of Articles 19 to 26

Benefit-sharing may be monetary or non-monetary (Article 19). Non-monetary modalities include biodiversity conservation projects at the site where the species occurs, technology transfer, making products available in the public domain, no-cost licensing, and training of human resources.

When the modality is monetary and the finished product or reproductive material results from access to genetic heritage, the user must pay **1% of annual net revenue** obtained from economic exploitation (Article 20). The amount may be reduced to as low as 0.1% through a sectoral agreement negotiated with the Union (Article 21). In non-monetary modalities, the sharing must equal 75% of the monetary amount, according to CGen criteria (Article 22).

The treatment of associated traditional knowledge distinguishes two situations:

- **Unidentifiable origin:** when it is not possible to identify the population holding the knowledge, benefit-sharing is necessarily monetary, at the same percentages (Articles 23 and 25). In this case, the benefit-sharing agreement is signed between the user and the Union, represented by the Ministry of the Environment.
- **Identifiable origin:** when the knowledge provider is identifiable, sharing is negotiated directly between the user and the provider, in a fair and equitable manner, with clauses on conditions, obligations, types, and duration of benefits (Article 24). It is conclusively presumed that there are other holders of the same knowledge, and they receive their share through the National Fund for Benefit-Sharing (FNRB).

Benefit-sharing agreements must specify the parties, the products exploited, the term, the modality, rights and responsibilities, the intellectual property clause, penalties, and the forum in Brazil (Article 26). Non-compliance subjects the user to administrative sanctions ranging from a warning to a fine, seizure of samples, and suspension of the manufacture and sale of the product (Article 27).

## SisGen: the registration system in practice

The electronic access registration is done in SisGen—the National System for the Management of Genetic Heritage and Associated Traditional Knowledge. SisGen was created by [Decree No. 8,772 of May 11, 2016](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2016/decreto/d8772.htm), which regulates Law 13.123/2015, and is operated by the Executive Secretariat of CGen.

In SisGen, the user declares activities of access to genetic heritage and associated traditional knowledge, the sending of samples for the provision of services abroad, the shipment of samples, and the economic exploitation of a finished product or reproductive material. The system also manages benefit-sharing agreements and the payment of amounts due.

For companies and researchers, the practical rule is: **all access to Brazilian genetic heritage must be registered in SisGen**, and the economic exploitation of a derived product requires the benefit-sharing agreement to be up to date. The registration is declaratory—the user is responsible for the accuracy of the information—and the system began full operation in November 2017, when the deadline for regularizing activities initiated before the law expired.

## What to watch for going forward

The late promulgation of the Nagoya Protocol (Decree 11.865/2023) closes a cycle of uncertainty: the treaty now has the force of domestic law, and Brazil is formally aligned with the international access and benefit-sharing regime. The points of attention for companies and research institutions are:

- **Continuous compliance:** register every new access in SisGen and keep economic exploitation information up to date. The fine for unregistered access and for failing to share benefits may reach R$ 10 million for legal entities (Article 27, § 5).
- **Sectoral agreements:** sectors that consider the 1% of net revenue disproportionate may seek sectoral agreements to reduce the rate to 0.1%—an instrument still little used, but provided for by law.
- **Relationship with industrial property law:** benefit-sharing does not prevent patenting, but the intellectual property clause is an essential element of the agreement. For biotechnology, this regime adds to other international frameworks—such as the [Budapest Treaty]({{< relref "posts/brasil-tratado-budapeste-patentes-biotecnologia/" >}}), which Brazil joined for the international deposit of microorganisms.

For a country that holds the world's greatest biodiversity, the biodiversity law is at once an instrument for protecting national heritage and for providing legal certainty for innovation. The Nagoya Protocol and Law 13.123/2015 form the framework that allows Brazil to charge for what nature offers—and the productive sector to innovate without risk.
