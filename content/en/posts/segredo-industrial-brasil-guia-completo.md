The Coca-Cola formula is one of the most valuable trade secrets in the world — and it has never been patented. Since 1886, it has been protected by a mechanism that requires no registration, has no expiration date, and publishes nothing: the trade secret. Meanwhile, thousands of Brazilian companies lose know-how every year because they believe the only way to protect an intellectual creation is to file a patent with INPI.

The truth is that Brazilian law protects confidential information — trade secrets, customer lists, algorithms, manufacturing processes — without any registration. The protection exists, but it depends on two things many companies neglect: knowing the legal basis and, above all, documenting the reasonable measures taken to keep the secret.

This guide explains what a trade secret is in Brazil, how it compares to a patent, what the legal basis is (LPI and TRIPS), and how to protect know-how in practice.

## What a trade secret is (and what the law requires)

A trade secret is any confidential information that has commercial value precisely because it is not public: a formula, a production process, unpublished software, a customer base, a pricing strategy, or research and development results.

The international framework is [Article 39 of TRIPS](https://www.wto.org/english/tratop_e/trips_e/t_agm3_e.htm), which defines three cumulative requirements for information to be protected as "undisclosed information":

| Requirement | Practical meaning |
|-----------|---------------------|
| **Secret** | Not generally known or readily accessible to circles that deal with this kind of information |
| **Commercial value** | Has economic value precisely because it is secret |
| **Reasonable measures** | The holder took reasonable measures to keep it confidential |

The third requirement is the most ignored — and the most decisive. If a company cannot demonstrate that it treated the information as a secret (access controls, confidentiality agreements, document classification), the legal protection falls apart. It is not enough to claim: you must prove that the secret was treated as such.

The economic importance of this topic has grown to the point of appearing in WIPO's global reports: worldwide investment in intangible assets — software, data, brands, R&D, and organizational capital, which includes know-how — [surpassed US$ 10 trillion for the first time in 2025](https://www.wipo.int/pressroom/en/articles/2026/article_0011.html), growing three times faster than tangible investment. A large share of that value is trade secret.

## Trade secret vs. patent: which one to choose?

The decision between patenting and keeping something secret is strategic and starts with a question: is the information patentable? Before making any choice, it is worth doing a [patent prior art search]({{< relref "posts/inpi-credenciamento-busca-anterioridade-patentes/" >}}) to understand the state of the art — especially because INPI pays for this service through accredited agents.

| Criterion | Patent | Trade secret |
|----------|---------|--------------------|
| Registration | Requires filing with INPI and examination | No registration; protection arises from secrecy |
| Disclosure | The application is published (18 months) and requires a sufficient description for reproduction | Nothing is published |
| Term | 20 years (invention) or 15 years (utility model) from filing | As long as the information remains secret |
| Protection against | Any unauthorized use, including by independent developers | Only improper acquisition/use (breach of confidentiality) |
| Cost | INPI fees, power of attorney, examination, annuities | Internal governance cost (contracts, IT, processes) |
| Risk | Application may be rejected; invention becomes public domain | Secret may leak or be discovered through reverse engineering |

The practical rule: a patent pays off when the invention is easily identifiable in the product (reverse engineering is possible) and the market justifies the 20-year exclusivity term. A trade secret pays off when the information is not detectable from the product (formulas, internal processes, algorithms, customer data) and the company has the ability to keep it under control.

There are cases in which a patent is simply not available — and secrecy becomes the only alternative. In biotechnology, for example, Brazil's accession to the [Budapest Treaty]({{< relref "posts/brasil-tratado-budapeste-patentes-biotecnologia/" >}}) made the deposit of the microorganism mandatory for descriptive sufficiency purposes: whoever files a biotech patent hands the biological material over to the public. If what matters is the process rather than the microorganism itself, secrecy may be the right choice.

## Legal basis in Brazil: LPI art. 195 and unfair competition

Brazil does not have a specific trade secret law, like the United States (Defend Trade Secrets Act) or the European Union (Directive 2016/943). Protection is achieved through a set of rules, with emphasis on the [Industrial Property Law (Law 9.279/96)](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/leis/l9279.htm).

The LPI treats trade secret violation as a **crime of unfair competition**. Article 195 provides, among other items:

- **XI** — disclosing, exploiting, or using, without authorization, confidential knowledge, information, or data usable in industry, commerce, or the provision of services, to which one gained access through a contractual or employment relationship, **even after the contract ends**;
- **XII** — disclosing, exploiting, or using, without authorization, knowledge or information to which one gained access through an employment contract, even after the contract ends;
- **XIV** — disclosing, exploiting, or using, without authorization, test results or other undisclosed data submitted to government entities as a condition for approving the commercialization of products (protection of regulatory data, which in Brazil still lacks full regulation).

The penalty is imprisonment from 3 months to 1 year, or a fine (art. 196). In the civil sphere, the injured holder may seek damages for losses, injunctive relief to stop the use, and even search and seizure of materials containing the violated information. It is worth remembering that the suppression of unfair competition is, under art. 2 of the LPI, one of the official mechanisms for protecting industrial property in the country — just as legitimate as patents and trademarks.

The weak point of the Brazilian system is fragmentation: besides the LPI, protection depends on contractual clauses (employment contracts, NDAs, confidentiality agreements in joint ventures) and on proving the secrecy measures. That is why internal governance is the true heart of protection.

## How to protect a trade secret in practice

Protecting know-how is designing a governance system, not signing a single document. In practice, the essential measures are:

1. **Non-disclosure agreements (NDAs)** — before sharing any sensitive information with suppliers, partners, investors, or consultants. The NDA must define what is confidential, the purpose of use, and what happens in case of breach.
2. **Clauses in employment contracts** — confidentiality, non-competition, and non-solicitation of customers/employees. Items XI and XII of art. 195 protect the company even after the employment relationship ends, but the contractual clause facilitates proof and expands the consequences.
3. **Physical and digital access control** — role-based permissions, separate environments for sensitive information, access logs, and blocking of downloads/exfiltration.
4. **Information classification** — marking documents as "confidential," restricting access to customer lists and databases.
5. **Employee offboarding procedures** — return of equipment and documents, immediate revocation of access, exit interviews, and reminders of confidentiality obligations.
6. **Documenting reasonable measures** — the TRIPS requirement demands proof. Keeping records of security policies, access controls, and signed contracts is what turns a secrecy claim into an enforceable right in court.
7. **Caution in technology transfer** — in licensing or know-how transfer contracts, precisely define what is being transferred, the confidentiality term, and what remains under the control of the transferor.

In M&A and due diligence operations, the trade secret is often the most valuable asset — and the most fragile. Data rooms must have per-document access control, and the NDA is a precondition for any visit or material delivery.

## The limits of trade secrets

It is essential to understand what a trade secret **does not** protect:

- **Independent development** — if a competitor reaches the same result on its own, without using the confidential information, there is no violation. A patent protects against this; a trade secret does not.
- **Reverse engineering** — taking apart a lawfully acquired product and discovering the process is not unlawful, except where a contractual clause states otherwise.
- **Accidental or involuntary disclosure** — if the information becomes public (through publication, a lecture, or a commercial presentation), the secret dies, and with it the protection. There is no way to "recover" secrecy.
- **Information obtained by legitimate means** — art. 195 punishes obtaining information through breach of confidentiality (contract, employment), not mere knowledge.

These limits reinforce the golden rule: a trade secret is a **behavioral** protection regime. It holds as long as the organization behaves like the owner of the secret.

## What to watch in Brazil

Two recent developments deserve attention from those who depend on know-how:

First, the reinforcement of institutional enforcement: [ABPI has joined the National Council to Combat Piracy and Intellectual Property Crimes (CNCP)](https://abpi.org.br/noticias/abpi-integra-o-conselho-nacional-de-combate-a-pirataria/), an advisory body of the Ministry of Justice created by Decree 9.875/2019. This expands the private sector's technical participation in enforcement policies — although CNCP's focus is piracy, smuggling, and counterfeiting, the strengthening of the enforcement ecosystem also benefits trade secret misappropriation cases.

Second, the debate over updating the LPI, which reaches three decades with [improvement proposals under discussion]({{< relref "posts/lpi-30-anos-propostas-aperfeicoamento-inpi/" >}}). A specific trade secret law, along the lines of the European directive, is one of the gaps pointed out by legal scholarship — but until then, art. 195 already provides a solid basis for those who structure protection correctly.

For the company, the practical checklist is short: (i) is the information secret and commercially valuable? (ii) are the reasonable secrecy measures documented? (iii) do the contracts cover employees, partners, and former collaborators? If the answer is yes to all three, the know-how is protected under Brazilian law — without registration, without fees, and without publication.
