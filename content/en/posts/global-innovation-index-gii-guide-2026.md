Every year, the World Intellectual Property Organization (WIPO) publishes the Global Innovation Index (GII), the benchmark that governments, development banks and companies use to measure the innovation performance of nearly 140 economies. Although it is often read as a simple ranking of countries, the GII is a far richer instrument: it combines more than 80 indicators — from R&D investment to high-tech trade and IP licensing — to explain what drives innovation in each country.

For anyone working in industrial property, patents and innovation, understanding how the index is built matters twice over. Intellectual property is not an isolated data point: it is one of the inputs the GII measures and, at the same time, one of the outputs it reflects. This guide explains what the GII is, how it is calculated, and what the 2026 edition says about Brazil.

## What the Global Innovation Index is

Launched in 2007, the GII has become the standard reference for benchmarking innovation ecosystems. It is not limited to patents or scientific publications: it assesses how well a country turns knowledge into economic and social value.

The index covers about 140 economies and is built with indicators grouped into two blocks:

- **Inputs** — the resources and conditions that enable innovation: institutions, infrastructure, human capital, and the market and business sophistication.
- **Outputs** — the results actually delivered: knowledge and technology outputs (where patents, scientific articles and licensing sit) and creative outputs.

The relationship between the two produces the **innovation efficiency indicator**, which compares whether a country gets more than its level of development and inputs would predict. Economies that consistently clear that threshold are called *overperformers*.

## How it is calculated and why IP matters

Among the output indicators, intellectual property sits at the centre. The GII uses variables such as:

- patent applications per capita,
- number of registered trademarks,
- indexed scientific articles,
- IP licensing and royalties,
- value of high- and medium-high-tech production.

So the index does not treat IP as an isolated legal formality: it treats it as a **thermometer of real innovative activity**. A country that patents little or licenses little IP tends to score lower on knowledge outputs, even with good infrastructure. For IP lawyers and consultants, this is a natural entry point: the index figures directly reflect the volume and quality of registered intangible assets.

No new economy joined the top 15 in 2026; the United Arab Emirates (25th) gained five places. Among the fastest risers over the past decade are China, India, Viet Nam, Türkiye, the Philippines, Morocco and Indonesia, with **Brazil, Mauritius, Saudi Arabia and Qatar also on upward trajectories**.

**Key macro numbers from the report**

- Global R&D expenditure grew more than 3.3% in real terms in 2025 and is projected to reach **US$ 3.4 trillion** in 2026, increasingly concentrated in middle-income economies.
- Corporate R&D grew 5.8% in real terms in 2025 to a record **US$ 1.5 trillion**, driven by software, AI and defence.
- Venture capital grew 28% to **US$ 510 billion** in 2025, the strongest annual gain since 2021. But investment is increasingly concentrated: **AI accounted for 53% of global VC deal value in 2025 and 77% in the first half of 2026**.
- Deep-science startups are now worth a combined **US$ 7.6 trillion** (more than 30,000 companies mapped since 2000), up 23% from 2024.

## Brazil in the GII 2026: the region's only outperformer

For Brazil, the 2026 edition offers two readings:

1. **Upward trajectory confirmed.** The release places Brazil, alongside Mauritius, Saudi Arabia and Qatar, among the economies with sustained improvements in innovation performance.
2. **Latin America's only outperformer.** Brazil remains the only economy in the region performing above what its level of development would predict. That does not mean leading the absolute ranking; it means converting inputs into results more efficiently than the average of its income group.

On deep science, the report highlights that growth since 2020 has been fastest in Central and Southern Asia (118%), Latin America and the Caribbean (71%) and Sub-Saharan Africa (65%), with **Türkiye, India, Malaysia, Brazil and South Africa** leading the middle-income group.

## Why this matters for IP practice

- **IP is an innovation indicator.** The patent and licensing metrics in the index confirm that protecting and managing intangible assets boosts measured competitiveness. This is not just legal compliance; it is industrial policy.
- **Brazil monetizes what it already has.** Its outperformer position suggests the problem is not scarce resources but under-use of existing ones. Improving the capacity to patent, license and negotiate IP — from research institutions, universities and SMEs — is a efficiency lever.
- **AI is redrawing the patent map.** With 77% of first-half venture capital concentrated in AI and generative-AI patenting having nearly tripled in two years, according to WIPO, whoever builds the ability to register and protect AI assets today will be better positioned as the field matures.

## Conclusion

The Global Innovation Index is not a prize or a fleeting news item: it is an annual — and cumulative — snapshot of how economies turn knowledge into value. For Brazil, the 2026 figure is encouraging but demanding: it keeps the country's status as a regional outperformer and a rising economy, against a backdrop where AI concentrates global investment and deep science is creating new industries at speed.

For the IP professional, the operational takeaway is immediate: intellectual property stops being a standalone legal topic and becomes a central competitiveness indicator. Strengthening patent, trademark and licensing management is not bureaucracy — it is the most direct way to improve the country's (and clients') standing in next year's index.
