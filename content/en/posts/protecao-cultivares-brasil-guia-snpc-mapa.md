A new soybean variety, a coffee cultivar more resistant to drought, or a forage grass with higher nutritional value: in Brazil, the legal protection of these plants **does not go through the INPI** and **is not a patent**. It is a system of its own — plant variety protection — governed by Law No. 9.456/1997 (the Plant Variety Protection Law, LPC in Portuguese) and administered by the National Plant Variety Protection Service (SNPC), part of the Ministry of Agriculture and Livestock (MAPA).

The confusion between cultivar and patent is one of the most frequent issues for anyone working with agribusiness innovation — and it is costly: applications filed with the wrong office, incomplete protection strategies, and, in the worst case, loss of rights through disclosure before filing. This guide covers the legal basis, the requirements, the step-by-step registration process, and the rights (and limits) of those who protect a plant variety in Brazil.

## A cultivar is not a patent: Brazil's sui generis system

The starting point is the Industrial Property Law (LPI). Article 18, III, of Law 9.279/96 excludes from patentability "all or part of living beings," with a single exception: transgenic microorganisms that meet the requirements of novelty, inventive step, and industrial application — and even in those cases, the sole paragraph of the provision expressly excludes "all or part of plants or animals."

In other words: **a plant variety as such cannot be patented in Brazil**. Whoever develops a new cultivar protects it through the Plant Variety Protection Certificate, which the LPC (Article 2) defines as the "sole form of protection of cultivars and the only right that may prevent the free use of plants or their reproductive or vegetative multiplication parts in the country." The certificate is considered movable property for all legal purposes.

| Aspect | Patent (LPI) | Plant variety protection (LPC) |
|--------|--------------|-------------------------------|
| Subject matter | Invention or utility model | Plant variety (cultivar) |
| Legal basis | Law 9.279/96 | Law 9.456/97 |
| Office | INPI | SNPC/MAPA |
| Requirements | Novelty, inventive step, industrial application | Novelty, distinctness, homogeneity, stability (DHE) |
| Term | 20 years (invention) / 15 years (utility model) | 15 years, or 18 for vines, fruit trees, forest and ornamental trees |
| Examination | Technical examination at INPI | DHE descriptors analyzed by SNPC |

Note that patent examination of biotechnological processes — for example, a genetic transformation method — remains with the INPI. But the **product** (the transgenic plant itself) is protected as a cultivar, and the **genetic construct** can be the subject of a transgenic microorganism patent within the limits of Article 18, III, of the LPI. In practice, major agricultural biotechnology companies combine both systems. The [Budapest Treaty]({{< relref "posts/brasil-tratado-budapeste-patentes-biotecnologia/" >}}) enters this equation when biological material is deposited for patent purposes.

## The legal basis: Law 9.456/97 and UPOV

The LPC implements in Brazil the International Convention for the Protection of New Varieties of Plants (UPOV), in its 1978 version, promulgated by [Decree No. 3.109/1999](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/decreto/D3109.htm). Brazil has been a [UPOV](https://www.upov.int/members/en/) member since 1999, which guarantees Brazilian breeders reciprocal treatment in other signatory countries.

The law defines a cultivar (Article 3, IV) as a variety of any genus or species of higher plant that is:
- clearly distinguishable from other known cultivars by a minimum margin of descriptors;
- homogeneous and stable with respect to its descriptors through successive generations;
- of a species usable by the agroforestry complex;
- described in specialized, publicly available and accessible publications.

**Component lines of hybrids** are also protectable. There is also the figure of the **essentially derived cultivar** — one derived from another protected cultivar without essential differences in the characteristics resulting from the genotype — which cannot be exploited without the authorization of the holder of the initial cultivar, even when the derivation involves breeding.

## Protection requirements: novelty and DHE

Two sets of requirements must be met:

1. **Commercial novelty (Article 3, V):** the cultivar must not have been offered for sale in Brazil for more than 12 months before the filing date (with specific rules for commercialization abroad).
2. **DHE:** distinctness, homogeneity, and stability — assessed through morphological, physiological, biochemical, or molecular descriptors that are genetically inherited (Article 3, II).

The analysis of descriptors is the core of the SNPC examination: it determines whether the cultivar is truly "new" against the bank of known cultivars and whether the characteristics remain stable across generations.

## Step-by-step registration at SNPC/MAPA

Plant variety registration is not done at the INPI, but at the SNPC, part of MAPA. The basic flow, set out in Articles 13 to 22 of the LPC:

1. **Application (Articles 13-14):** a signed request by the breeder (or by an attorney domiciled in Brazil, for foreign holders), referring to a **single cultivar**, containing the botanical species, the cultivar name, genetic origin, and a descriptive report.
2. **Publication (Article 16):** the application is published in extract form within 60 calendar days.
3. **Opposition (Article 16, sole paragraph):** third parties have 90 days to oppose.
4. **Descriptor examination (Articles 17-22):** the SNPC checks formal compliance and analyzes the DHE descriptors; they cannot be modified after publication, except to correct errors or meet formal requirements.
5. **Provisional Protection Certificate:** granted in the early stage, it ensures protection from the publication of the application (the term runs from the grant of the provisional certificate — Article 11).
6. **Definitive Protection Certificate:** granted after examination approval, consolidating the right.

An important practical detail: when the variety results from an employment or service contract, the application **must name all breeders** who participated (Article 5, §3) — the same care with ownership that applies to employee inventions under the LPI. And if the development was cooperative, protection can be sought jointly or individually, with the qualification of each co-breeder (Article 5, §2).

## What the certificate grants — and what it does not cover

The certificate holder has the exclusive right to commercial reproduction, offering for sale, import, export, and storage of propagating material in Brazil. But the LPC preserves important balances that confuse those coming from the patent regime:

- **Farm-saved seed (Article 10, I):** farmers can reserve and plant seeds for their own use, without paying royalties.
- **Use as food/raw material (Article 10, II):** the harvested product can be used or sold as food, provided it is not for reproductive purposes.
- **Research and breeding (Article 10, III):** the cultivar can be used as a source of genetic variation.
- **Smallholders and family farming (Article 10, IV and V):** multiplication of seeds for donation or exchange among small producers and, under Article 19 of Law 10.696/2003, by family farmers qualified under Law 11.326/2006.

The protection term is **15 years** from the grant of the Provisional Certificate, and **18 years** for vines, fruit trees, forest trees, and ornamental trees, including their rootstocks (Article 11). Once the term expires, the cultivar falls into the **public domain** and no other right can prevent its free use (Article 12).

The certificate is also a tradable asset: ownership can be transferred by inter vivos act or succession, and licenses, changes, and other acts must be recorded on the certificate (Articles 23-24) — a process analogous to the registration of technology transfer contracts at the INPI, but with the record made before the SNPC.

## Enforcement: what happens to unauthorized use

Article 37 of the LPC is the backbone of enforcement: whoever sells, offers for sale, reproduces, imports, exports, packages, or stores propagating material of a protected cultivar without the holder's authorization is obliged to **compensate**, in addition to having the material **seized** and paying a **fine of 20% of the commercial value** of the seized material — without prejudice to the characterization of the crime of violation of breeder's rights. In case of repeat offense, the fine percentage is doubled.

In practice, an agribusiness protection strategy combines: (i) a cultivar certificate at SNPC for the variety, (ii) a patent for the process/technology when applicable, (iii) well-recorded licensing contracts, and (iv) seed market monitoring — a problem that also affects trademark counterfeiting enforcement in retail and digital marketplaces.

## What to watch

- **File with the right office:** cultivar is SNPC/MAPA; patent is INPI. Filing with the wrong counter costs months.
- **Do not disclose before filing:** the commercial novelty window is 12 months of offering for sale in Brazil; plan scientific publication or commercial launch after filing.
- **Name all breeders** in the application (Article 5, §3) to avoid ownership disputes later.
- **Record licenses and transfers** on the certificate to give publicity and opposability to third parties.
- **Essentially derived cultivars** still depend on the authorization of the holder of the initial cultivar.

Plant variety protection is the main instrument for appropriating innovation in Brazilian agribusiness — one of the sectors where the country invests most in genetic improvement. Understanding the difference between this regime and patents is the first step toward a complete IP strategy, which can be combined with other forms of protection such as [geographical indications]({{< relref "posts/guia-indicacoes-geograficas-inpi-brasil/" >}}) for agri-food products.
