In August 2026, INPI released a study with a figure that few people outside the patent world can grasp: **6,300 patent applications related to sustainable agriculture filed in Brazil since 2012**. The survey is part of the Technological Radars, a series of free public studies by the Institute that maps patent filings by sector — and that remains one of the most underused competitive intelligence tools in the Brazilian innovation ecosystem.

This guide explains what the Technological Radars are, what the collection covers, how to interpret the data, and how to use them in practice to guide R&D, investment, and IP strategy decisions.

## What is an INPI Technological Radar

A Technological Radar is a technology prospecting study that analyzes **patent applications filed in Brazil** in a given technical field. Rather than describing technologies individually, it treats the set of patent documents as a statistical database: who files, where applicants come from, which fields are growing, which are shrinking.

This works because a patent is, in practice, the most structured and public way for a company to declare where it is investing in innovation. A patent application reveals the technology, the applicant, the country of origin, and the date — data that, when aggregated, form a map of a sector's inventive effort.

The radars are produced by INPI based on the International Patent Classification and bibliometric analyses, with support from partners such as universities and research institutes. The result is published in three formats: the **full report** (PDF), the **executive summary**, and, in more recent editions, an **interactive data dashboard** that lets you filter information by interest.

## Why the radars matter

For Brazilian companies, startups, and research institutions, the radars solve an expensive problem: access to patent intelligence. Commercial databases such as Orbit, Questel, and Derwent charge subscriptions of tens of thousands of reais per year. The radars deliver a relevant layer of that intelligence **for free**, with data extracted from INPI's own collection.

They help answer practical questions such as:

- **What technology are competitors filing in?** The radar lists the main applicants in each field.
- **Where is the sector's technological frontier?** Fields with accelerated proportional growth indicate where the market is moving.
- **Is there room for Brazilian participation?** The ratio between national and foreign applicants reveals technological dependency and opportunities.
- **Who are the potential research partners?** Universities and S&T institutions often appear among the main domestic applicants.

The [INPI Technological Radars](https://www.gov.br/inpi/pt-br/assuntos/informacao/radares-tecnologicos) page brings together more than 40 studies, with editions published since 2014. Coverage is broad and spans strategic sectors:

## Case study: the Sustainable Agriculture Radar

The radar released in August 2026, prepared under INPI's Green Technologies Observatory, is a good example of the kind of insight these studies provide. The figures, taken from the [executive summary](https://www.gov.br/inpi/pt-br/assuntos/informacao/otv-resumo-executivo-agricultura-sustentavel_-versao-final.pdf), tell a clear story:

- **6,300 applications** filed in Brazil since 2012, around 1.8% of all annual filings received by INPI.
- **Concentration in three fields:** sustainable pesticides (52% of filings), biofertilizers (41%), and digital agriculture (34%). Together, the three account for 89% of the inventive effort — an application may belong to more than one field, which is why the percentages exceed 100%.
- **Sustainable pesticides lead** across all time periods; **digital agriculture is the most dynamic frontier**, the field that gained the most proportional share in the period.
- **Foreign predominance:** foreign applicants account for 82% of filings, and the US leads all analyzed technical fields, with more than 40% of filings.
- **Embrapa is the only Brazilian applicant among the top 15** in the sector.
- **Brazil is the second largest source** of technology filed with INPI in almost all fields — except for new plants, where the US concentrates 59% of filings.
- **National inventive capacity is concentrated in the South and Southeast (79%)**, with São Paulo alone accounting for 31% of inventions by Brazilian applicants. The national share of the total rose from 14% to 20% in ten years, driven by biofertilizers and sustainable pesticides, with a strong presence of universities and public institutions.

For an agribusiness innovation manager, this reading has direct implications: there is heated technological demand in bioinputs, the Brazilian market is strategic for multinationals (which explains the foreign volume), and there is a national opportunity window in digital agriculture, where proportional growth is higher.

## How to use the radars in practice

The usage flow is simple and can be incorporated into the routine of any IP or innovation area:

1. **Access the official page** of [Technological Radars](https://www.gov.br/inpi/pt-br/assuntos/informacao/radares-tecnologicos) and identify the study for your sector. If there is no radar for your area, reports from neighboring sectors still serve as a methodological reference.
2. **Download the full report and the executive summary.** The summary brings the main figures; the full report details the methodology and the tables by technical field.
3. **Explore the interactive data dashboard**, when available. In recent editions, it allows filtering by applicant, country of origin, technical field, and period.
4. **Extract the three readings that matter:** trends (which fields are growing), competitors (who files and from where), and gaps (fields with low Brazilian participation — natural candidates for promotion policies or strategic positioning).
5. **Cross-reference with other sources.** The radars show the aggregate landscape; to evaluate a specific application or an individual technology, combine it with a [patent search in international databases]({{< relref "posts/busca-internacional-patentes-patentscope-espacenet-google-2026/" >}}) and with a search of INPI's collection.

## Limitations you need to know

The radars are excellent, but they have limitations that need to be factored into the analysis:

- **Fixed time frame:** the data reflects the period studied up to the publication date. The scenario changes — it is worth repeating the analysis when a new edition of the same sector is released.
- **National scope:** the focus is patenting in Brazil, not worldwide. For a global view, the starting point is WIPO's indicators, such as the [global PCT patent map]({{< relref "posts/mapa-global-patentes-pct-2025-brasil/" >}}).
- **Overlapping percentages:** because an application can be classified in more than one technical field, the slices add up to more than 100% — that is not an error, it is a feature of the methodology.
- **Does not replace a prior art search:** the radar is a landscape prospecting tool, not an analysis of a specific application. For filing decisions, the individual [prior art search]({{< relref "posts/inpi-credenciamento-busca-anterioridade-patentes/" >}}) remains a mandatory step before [filing a patent]({{< relref "posts/guia-deposito-patente-inpi-2026/" >}}).

## Conclusion

INPI's Technological Radars are probably the best free patent intelligence resource available in Brazil — and they remain underused outside the circle of IP specialists. Each new edition is an opportunity to understand, with official data and public methodology, where a sector's inventive effort is heading.

For those who work with innovation, the recommended move is simple: find the radar for your sector, read the executive summary, explore the data dashboard, and turn the numbers into decisions — for R&D, partnerships, or positioning. The next radar may be closer to your market than you imagine.
