In August 2026, INPI released a study with a number that few people outside the patent world can grasp: **6.3 thousand patent applications related to sustainable agriculture filed in Brazil since 2012**. The survey is part of the Technological Radars, a series of free public studies by the Institute that maps patenting by sector — and which remains one of the most underutilized competitive intelligence tools in the Brazilian innovation ecosystem.

This guide explains what the Technological Radars are, what the collection covers, how to interpret the data, and how to use them in practice to guide R&D, investment, and IP strategy decisions.

## What is an INPI Technological Radar

A Technological Radar is a technology foresight study that analyzes **patent applications filed in Brazil** in a given technical field. Instead of describing technologies individually, it treats the set of patent documents as a statistical database: who files, where filers come from, which fields are growing, which are shrinking.

This works because a patent is, in practice, the most structured and public way for a company to declare where it is investing in innovation. A patent application reveals the technology, the applicant, the country of origin, and the date — data that, when aggregated, form a map of a sector's inventive effort.

The radars are prepared by INPI based on the international patent classification and bibliometric analyses, with support from partners such as universities and research institutes. The result is published in three formats: the **full report** (PDF), the **executive summary**, and, in the most recent editions, an **interactive data dashboard** that lets you filter information by interest.

## Why the radars matter

For Brazilian companies, startups, and research institutions, the radars solve an expensive problem: access to patent intelligence. Commercial databases such as Orbit, Questel, and Derwent charge subscriptions of tens of thousands of reais per year. The radars deliver a relevant layer of that intelligence **for free**, with data extracted from INPI's own collection.

They can answer practical questions such as:

- **What technology are competitors filing in?** The radar lists the main applicants in each field.
- **Where is the sector's technological frontier?** Fields with accelerated proportional growth indicate where the market is moving.
- **Is there room for Brazilian participation?** The ratio between domestic and foreign applicants reveals technological dependency and opportunities.
- **Who are the potential research partners?** Universities and science and technology institutions often appear among the top domestic applicants.

## The available collection

The [INPI Technological Radars](https://www.gov.br/inpi/pt-br/assuntos/informacao/radares-tecnologicos) page brings together more than 40 studies, with editions published since 2014. The coverage is broad and spans strategic sectors:

## Case study: the Sustainable Agriculture Radar

The radar released in August 2026, prepared under the INPI Green Technologies Observatory, is a good example of the kind of reading these studies enable. The numbers, extracted from the [executive summary](https://www.gov.br/inpi/pt-br/assuntos/informacao/otv-resumo-executivo-agricultura-sustentavel_-versao-final.pdf), tell a clear story:

- **6.3 thousand patent applications** filed in Brazil since 2012, about 1.8% of all annual filings received by INPI.
- **Concentration in three fields:** sustainable crop protection products (52% of filings), biofertilizers (41%), and digital agriculture (34%). Together, the three account for 89% of the inventive effort — one application may belong to more than one field, which is why the percentages exceed 100%.
- **Foreign predominance:** foreign applicants account for 82% of filings, and the U.S. leads all analyzed technological fields, with more than 40% of filings.
- **Embrapa is the only Brazilian applicant among the top 15** in the sector.
- **Brazil is the second largest source** of technology filed at INPI in almost all fields — except in new plants, where the U.S. concentrates 59% of filings.
- **Domestic inventive capacity is concentrated in the South and Southeast (79%)**, with São Paulo alone accounting for 31% of Brazilian applicants' inventions. The national share of the total rose from 14% to 20% in ten years, driven by biofertilizers and sustainable crop protection products, with a strong presence of universities and public institutes.

For an agribusiness innovation manager, this reading has direct implications: there is heated technological demand in bioinputs, the Brazilian market is strategic for multinationals (which explains the foreign volume), and there is a window of opportunity for Brazil in digital agriculture, where proportional growth is greater.

## How to use the radars in practice

The workflow is simple and can be incorporated into the routine of any IP or innovation area:

1. **Access the official page** of [Technological Radars](https://www.gov.br/inpi/pt-br/assuntos/informacao/radares-tecnologicos) and identify the study for your sector. If there is no radar for your area, reports from neighboring sectors can still serve as a methodological reference.
2. **Download the full report and the executive summary.** The summary contains the main numbers; the full report details the methodology and the tables by technical field.
3. **Explore the interactive data dashboard**, when available. In recent editions, it lets you filter by applicant, country of origin, technological field, and period.
4. **Extract the three relevant readings:** trends (which fields are growing), competitors (who files and from where), and gaps (fields with low Brazilian participation — natural candidates for support policies or strategic positioning).
5. **Cross-reference with other sources.** The radars show the aggregate panorama; to evaluate a specific application or an individual technology, combine it with a [patent search in international databases]({{< relref "posts/busca-internacional-patentes-patentscope-espacenet-google-2026/" >}}) and with a consultation of INPI's collection.

## Limitations you need to know

The radars are excellent, but they have limitations that need to be part of the analysis:

- **Fixed time window:** the data reflect the period studied up to the publication date. The landscape changes — it is worth repeating the reading when a new edition for the same sector is released.
- **National scope:** the focus is patenting in Brazil, not worldwide. For a global view, the starting point is WIPO's indicators, such as the [global PCT patent map]({{< relref "posts/mapa-global-patentes-pct-2025-brasil/" >}}).
- **Overlapping percentages:** since one application can be classified in more than one technical field, the shares add up to more than 100% — it is not an error, it is a characteristic of the methodology.
- **It does not replace prior art search:** the radar is scenario prospecting, not analysis of a specific application. For filing decisions, an individual [prior art search]({{< relref "posts/inpi-credenciamento-busca-anterioridade-patentes/" >}}) remains a mandatory step before [filing a patent]({{< relref "posts/guia-deposito-patente-inpi-2026/" >}}).

## Conclusion

The INPI Technological Radars are probably the best free patent intelligence resource available in Brazil — and they continue to be underused outside the circle of IP specialists. Each new edition is an opportunity to understand, with official data and public methodology, where a sector's inventive effort is heading.

For those who work with innovation, the recommended move is simple: find your sector's radar, read the executive summary, explore the data dashboard, and turn the numbers into a decision — whether R&D, partnership, or positioning. The next radar may be closer to your market than you imagine.
