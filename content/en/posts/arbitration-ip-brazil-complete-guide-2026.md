Disputes involving patents, trademarks, trade secrets and technology contracts are among the most expensive and slow-moving cases in the Brazilian courts. When the subject matter is technical — a biotech patent, a royalty clause, a trademark coexistence agreement — arbitration has moved from a niche alternative to a strategic instrument.

Interest in the topic is growing. In 2026, IAM published the [fourth edition of the Guide to IP Arbitration](https://www.iam-media.com/guide/the-guide-ip-arbitration/fourth-edition), CSD-ABPI (the Brazilian IP dispute resolution center) marked 15 years of operation, and the 5th CSD-ABPI Moot brought the debate on arbitral jurisdiction in trademark and trade dress disputes to students and law firms across the country.

This guide covers what actually matters in practice: what can and cannot be arbitrated in IP in Brazil, why use arbitration, which institutions to choose, and how to structure the arbitration clause.

## What arbitration is and the legal basis in Brazil

Arbitration is a dispute resolution method in which the parties choose impartial arbitrators — instead of a state court judge — to decide the conflict. The decision (arbitral award) is final, is not subject to appeal before the courts and, if it orders payment, constitutes an enforceable title.

The legal basis is [Law 9.307/1996](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/leis/l9307.htm) (Brazilian Arbitration Act), declared constitutional by the Supreme Federal Court (STF) in 2001 (SE 5.206-7/Spain). Article 1 sets the central criterion: parties **capable of contracting may use arbitration to settle disputes involving disposable patrimonial rights**.

Following the reform introduced by [Law 13.129/2015](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2015/lei/l13129.htm), section 1 of Article 1 now expressly allows **direct and indirect public administration** to use arbitration for disputes over disposable patrimonial rights — a decisive point for contracts with science and technology institutions (ICTs) and research institutes, as discussed below.

## What can be arbitrated in IP: disposable patrimonial rights

Arbitrability in IP is not about "the industrial property right itself" but about the **disposable patrimonial rights** arising from contracts and relationships between parties. In practice, the following are arbitrable:

| Type of dispute | Examples |
|----------------|----------|
| Licensing and royalties | Amounts due, interpretation of clauses, termination |
| Technology transfer | Assignment agreements, know-how, technical assistance |
| Distribution and franchise agreements | Trademark use, territory, exclusivity terms |
| Trademark coexistence | Coexistence agreements between similar signs |
| Confidentiality and trade secrets | NDA breach, misuse of know-how |
| Unfair competition | Misappropriation of trade dress in a contractual context |
| Domain names | .br disputes (SACI-Adm) and gTLDs (UDRP) |
| IP asset valuation | Pricing, valuation in M&A |

The logic is the same that governs any arbitration: if the right can be the subject of a transaction between the parties, it can be arbitrated. Disputes **between private parties** over IP contracts are, as a rule, fully arbitrable — including matters involving technical patent analysis, as long as the conflict has a contractual origin.

## What CANNOT be arbitrated: validity before the INPI

The most important limit concerns the **validity of the registration** granted by the INPI (Brazilian Patent and Trademark Office). Nullity of a patent or trademark is not a disposable right: it involves public interest and the INPI is a necessary party.

- **Patents:** the [nullity action (Article 56 of the Brazilian Industrial Property Law — LPI)](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/leis/l9279.htm) may be filed at any time during the patent's term, by the INPI or by any person with a legitimate interest, and is brought before the **Federal Courts**, with mandatory intervention by the INPI (Article 57).
- **Trademarks:** a registration granted contrary to the law is null (Article 165 of the LPI), and nullity may be declared administratively by the INPI itself or judicially.

This does not mean validity is completely outside arbitration: in **disputes between private parties**, arbitrators may analyze validity as an **incidental question** (for example, to decide whether a licensee must pay royalties for a patent it claims is invalid), as long as the decision produces effects only between the parties — never erga omnes, as in a judicial nullity action. This is exactly the type of discussion raised by the 5th CSD-ABPI Moot case: how far does arbitral jurisdiction go when contract and registration intersect?

## Why arbitrate IP disputes

| Advantage | Practical impact |
|-----------|------------------|
| **Specialization** | Arbitrators with technical experience (chemists, engineers, trademark specialists) understand the subject without relying on lengthy expert reports |
| **Confidentiality** | The procedure is confidential — protecting trade secrets, portfolio strategies and royalty figures that a public lawsuit would expose |
| **Speed** | Deadlines set by the parties and the institution; an award in months, not years |
| **International enforcement** | Arbitral awards circulate far more easily than court judgments: Brazil joined the New York Convention in 2002 ([Decree 4.311/2002](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/decreto/2002/d4311.htm)), and foreign arbitral awards are recognized by the STJ |
| **Finality** | No appeal: the award can only be set aside on narrow grounds (Article 32 of Law 9.307/96) |

The international enforcement point deserves emphasis: in disputes between Brazilian and foreign companies (common in technology licensing and global distribution contracts), arbitration under an international institution's rules is often the only route that gives real enforcement security outside Brazil.

## Institutions: where to arbitrate in Brazil and abroad

### CSD-ABPI — the IP-specialized center

The [ABPI Dispute Resolution Center (CSD-ABPI)](https://csd-abpi.org.br/) is Brazil's main ADR hub specialized in intellectual property, with 15 years of operation. Its structure includes:

- **CArb-ABPI** — arbitration chamber with its own rules, arbitrator list and published fees
- **CMed-ABPI** — mediation chamber (procedures updated as of October 2025)
- **CASD-ND** — domain name chamber, administering the [SACI-Adm](https://csd-abpi.org.br/) system under an agreement with NIC.br for ".br" domain disputes
- **CDD-ABPI** — digital law chamber, offering online mediation, accelerated digital arbitration and expert determination

CSD-ABPI also publishes reference works on the topic, including specialized IP and biotech arbitration, trade secrets disputes and the discussion on **SEPs, FRAND and arbitration** — a hot topic for those working with standard-essential patents.

### Other chambers in Brazil

CAM-CCBC (São Paulo), CAMARB (Belo Horizonte), FGV (Rio/São Paulo) and CBMA (São Paulo) administer arbitrations involving IP within broader commercial disputes. The choice depends on the profile of the conflict: for predominantly commercial contract disputes with ancillary IP, a general chamber works; for a 100% IP subject matter, CSD-ABPI offers the specialization.

### International: WIPO Arbitration and Mediation Center

The [WIPO AMC](https://www.wipo.int/amc/en/) is the international center specialized in IP and technology disputes, headquartered in Geneva. It offers:

- **Mediation** — a consensual process assisted by a mediator
- **Arbitration and Expedited Arbitration** — binding and final decision
- **Expert Determination** — for specific technical questions, such as IP asset valuation or royalty rate setting
- **Domain name disputes** — global leader in administering cases under the UDRP

WIPO clauses are standard in international licensing and technology transfer contracts involving Brazil.

## Step by step: structuring arbitration in IP contracts

**1. Choose the right ADR model.** Mediation first (preserves the commercial relationship), arbitration for a final decision, or expert determination for an isolated technical question (e.g., royalty valuation).

**2. Draft the arbitration clause with precision.** The clause should state: (i) institution and rules (e.g., CSD-ABPI or WIPO); (ii) seat and language of arbitration; (iii) number of arbitrators and appointment method; (iv) governing law. Generic clauses ("disputes will be resolved by arbitration") trigger incidents that delay everything.

**3. Define the scope.** Make clear the clause covers contractual disputes, including interpretation, termination and amounts — and, if desired, the incidental analysis of validity between the parties.

**4. Anticipate confidentiality.** The clause should provide for confidentiality of the proceedings, documents and award, including for arbitrators and experts.

**5. Prepare enforcement.** For international contracts, prefer a seat in a New York Convention signatory country (virtually all relevant jurisdictions) and check whether recognition by the STJ is needed.

## Arbitration with the public sector: ICTs and the Innovation Law

Since Law 13.129/2015, universities, research institutes and other public administration entities may arbitrate disputes over disposable patrimonial rights. This matters for the innovation ecosystem: [technology transfer contracts registered with the INPI]({{< relref "posts/guia-registro-contratos-transferencia-tecnologia-inpi-2026/" >}}), co-ownership agreements and technology procurement (encomendas) with ICTs can provide for arbitration — a real alternative to the slowness of enforcement against public entities in royalty and ownership disputes.

## What to watch in the coming years

1. **SEPs and FRAND** — setting fair, reasonable and non-discriminatory rates for standard-essential patents (5G, IoT) is increasingly moving to specialized arbitration, as already happens in Europe and the US
2. **Digital disputes** — e-commerce, domain names and data are generating conflicts that digital law chambers (such as CDD-ABPI) absorb through accelerated procedures
3. **Growth of the Brazilian market** — the maturing of CSD-ABPI, arbitrator training via moots and regulatory predictability should expand the use of arbitration in licensing and trademark coexistence
