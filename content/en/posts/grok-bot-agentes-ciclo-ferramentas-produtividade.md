In August 2026, xAI launched [Grok Bot](https://x.ai/news/introducing-grok-bot) in beta: a team of "always-on" AI agents, each with its own cloud computer, that sign in to the tools you already use, carry out end-to-end tasks, and talk to each other in threads — a "chief of staff" bot coordinating specialists for inbox, expenses, recruiting, or bug fixing. In the official presentation, it looks like the future of work.

But I've seen this movie before. A few years ago, the same enthusiasm surrounded Obsidian, Notion, and Todoist: each presented itself as the definitive hub of your digital life, with plugins, automations, and a passionate community. None of them disappeared, but they stopped being the center — the productivity market fragmented again, and what was "the platform" became a feature inside other platforms.

The risk of Grok Bot is not technical, it's strategic. If the pattern repeats, within a few months OpenAI, Google, or Microsoft will incorporate "a team of agents that talk to each other" as just another feature — and whoever migrated their real workflow to Grok Bot is left with the rework of switching tools again, rebuilding routines, and relearning. Worse: you give a third-party company access to your accounts, emails, and CRM, and what the bots learned about the way you work stays locked in a proprietary system over which you have no control — the same [hidden cost of data]({{< relref "posts/modelos-ia-abertos-vs-fronteira-dados-2026/" >}}) we already discussed here.

None of this means Grok Bot is bad — specialized agents with their own computer are a genuine advance. But the question worth asking before adopting it is the same one we should have asked with productivity tools: does this solve a problem that will still exist two years from now, or am I just jumping to the tool of the moment? The pattern of today's AI market is that of yesterday's productivity tools: lots of promise, quick adoption by competitors, and oblivion.
