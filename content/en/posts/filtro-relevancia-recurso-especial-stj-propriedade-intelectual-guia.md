Since September 2026, taking an Intellectual Property (IP) matter to the Superior Court of Justice (STJ) has become more difficult — and more strategic. The requirement of the **federal issue relevance filter** ceased to be theoretical and became practically effective for every special appeal (REsp) filed in the country. For law firms working with trademarks, patents, utility models, and industrial designs, this demands rethinking how to construct the appeal from the initial petition, not just at the appellate stage.

This guide explains what the filter is, who regulates it, which IP cases fit into presumed and non-presumed relevance, and how to build a strategy that survives the new admissibility requirement — including the controversy over its interaction with Súmula 7.

## What is the federal issue relevance filter

The filter is a requirement for the admissibility of special appeals originally created by **Constitutional Amendment 125/2022**, which added §3º to article 105 of the Federal Constitution. The logic is the same as that of general repercussion in the STF: the appellant needs to demonstrate that the federal law issue discussed in the process is **relevant** — that is, that it transcends the subjective interests of the parties — under penalty of the appeal not being heard.

EC 125/2022 was born to address a structural problem: the colossal volume of special appeals that overloads the STJ. Without a filter, the court had to judge thousands of repetitive cases that add nothing to jurisprudence. The amendment provided for the mechanism, and the regulation came with **Law 15.484/2026**, published on 08/04/2026 and in force since **09/04/2026** for judgments published after this date.

For IP attorneys, the central point is: actions for patent infringement, nullity, trademark opposition, forfeiture, and moral damages resulting from IP rights infringement rarely fit into the hypotheses of presumed relevance. Most will have to demonstrate the concrete transcendence of the cause — and this profoundly changes how the appeal is drafted.

## Presumed relevance: cases that pass "automatically"

§3º of article 105 of the CF lists the hypotheses in which relevance is **presumed**. In these cases, it is sufficient for the appellant to subsume their case to one of the legal hypotheses to overcome the requirement:

- **Criminal actions** (inciso I);
- **Administrative improbity actions** (inciso II);
- **Actions whose cause value exceeds 500 minimum wages** (inciso III);
- **Actions that may lead to ineligibility** (inciso IV);
- **Cases in which the appealed judgment contradicts the dominant jurisprudence of the STJ** (inciso V).

In the IP universe, the most accessible criterion is the **value of the cause above 500 minimum wages** (in November 2026, the minimum wage is R$ 1,518, which places the ceiling at about **R$ 759 thousand**). Patent infringement actions and trademark disputes with expressive values frequently cross this threshold. Therefore, specialists recommend treating the **challenge to the value of the cause with redoubled attention** — it can determine access to the STJ itself, as pointed out by Union attorney Rodrigo Becker in an ABPI seminar.

Important: even when relevance is presumed, the **preliminary plea of relevance is mandatory in all cases**. The appellant cannot omit it on the grounds that the hypothesis is already presumed by law — they must formally demonstrate it.

## Non-presumed relevance: the four criteria for most IP cases

For the vast majority of IP processes — which are not criminal, do not involve improbity, do not exceed the value of 500 minimum wages, and do not contradict dominant jurisprudence — relevance needs to be **demonstrated**. The new article **1.035-A, §1º, of the CPC** (introduced by Law 15.484/2026) requires the appellant to prove the transcendence of the issue under **four legal criteria**, isolated or combined:

1.  **Economic** — the decision has relevant impacts on the economy, transcending the individual interest of the company;
2.  **Political** — the issue touches on public policies or institutional values;
3.  **Legal** — the thesis is capable of settling understanding on a topic with repercussion in other processes;
4.  **Social** — the decision affects a significant number of people or the community.

In the IP sector, Becker highlighted that the **social criterion concentrates more than 70% of cases** in which non-presumed relevance is recognized. And there is an important caveat for trademarks: **the value of a trademark, in isolation, is not enough** for the economic criterion. It is necessary to demonstrate that the decision has broad impacts on the economy and society — something very different from simply pointing out that the brand is worth millions.

## Interaction with Súmula 7: the open debate

One of the most sensitive points today is the relationship between the new requirement and **Súmula 7 of the STJ**, which prevents the reexamination of evidence in the superior court (used to dismiss appeals). The question under discussion in the courts: should the obstacle of Súmula 7 be applied **already in the analysis of relevance**, or should it be initially set aside so that relevance can be examined directly?

The outcome of this issue will have a decisive impact on the future of IP litigation in the country, because many trademark and patent actions depend on the framing of evidence — and the combination "lack of relevance + prohibited reexamination of evidence" can permanently close the STJ's door to the appellant. Follow the related judgments, as jurisprudence is still forming.

## How to build the appellate strategy in IP

With the new filter, special appeals in IP matters cease to be an "automatic appeal" and become a thesis document. Recommended practices:

-   **Declare the preliminary plea of relevance in all cases**, even in those of legal presumption — omission may lead to non-acknowledgment;
-   **Target the value of the cause from the initial petition**: if it is feasible and fair to raise the value beyond 500 minimum wages, access to the STJ becomes considerably simpler;
-   **Combine criteria in the preliminary plea** (e.g., economic + social), instead of betting on just one;
-   **Structure the appeal to settle recurrent theses**, such as requests for moral damages in IP actions — a thesis of collective interest strengthens the legal and social criteria;
-   **Prequestioning remains valid**: the absence of a decided cause (prequestioning) continues to be an obstacle, and the strategy of alleging violation of art. 1.022 of the CPC (clarification embargoes to remedy omission) needs to dialogue with the new requirement.

The relevance filter places IP in a terrain where the **quality of the thesis** matters more than procedural persistence. Understanding the criteria and drafting the appeal with demonstrated transcendence is, today, a condition for reaching the superior court.

## Points to monitor

-   **Forming jurisprudence** in the STJ on the application of the filter to IP cases and on its interaction with Súmula 7;
-   **Publication date of the appealed judgment**: the filter applies to judgments published after 09/04/2026;
-   **Administrative statements from the STJ** on the subject (such as those that set the vacatio legis), which detail practical application.
