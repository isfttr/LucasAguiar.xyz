Disputes involving patents, trademarks, trade secrets, and technology contracts are among the most expensive and time-consuming in the Brazilian Judiciary. When the subject matter is technical — a biotechnology patent, a royalty clause, a trademark coexistence agreement — arbitration has ceased to be a niche alternative and has become a strategic tool.

Interest in the topic is growing: in 2026, IAM published the [fourth edition of the Guide to IP Arbitration](https://www.iam-media.com/guide/the-guide-ip-arbitration/fourth-edition), CSD-ABPI completed 15 years of operation, and the 5th CSD-ABPI Moot brought the debate on arbitral jurisdiction in trademark and trade dress disputes to students and law firms across the country.

This guide covers what really matters in practice: what can and cannot be arbitrated in IP in Brazil, why to use arbitration, which institutions to choose, and how to structure the arbitration clause.

## What is arbitration and the legal basis in Brazil

Arbitration is a dispute resolution method in which the parties choose impartial arbitrators — rather than a state court judge — to decide the conflict. The decision (arbitral award) is final, is not subject to appeal to the Judiciary, and, if it is condemnatory, constitutes an enforceable instrument.

The legal basis is [Law 9.307/1996](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/leis/l9307.htm) (Arbitration Law), declared constitutional by the STF in 2001 (SE 5.206-7/Spain). Article 1 defines the central criterion: arbitration may be used by **persons capable of contracting, to resolve disputes concerning disposable patrimonial rights**.

With the reform introduced by [Law 13.129/2015](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/_ato2015-2018/2015/lei/l13129.htm), Article 1, §1 began expressly allowing **direct and indirect public administration** to use arbitration for conflicts over disposable patrimonial rights — a decisive point for contracts with ICTs and research institutes, as we will see below.

## What can be arbitrated in IP: disposable patrimonial rights

Arbitrability in IP is not about the "industrial property right itself", but about the **disposable patrimonial rights** that arise from contracts and relationships between parties. In practice, the following are arbitrable:

| Dispute type | Examples |
|----------------|----------|
| Licensing and royalties | Amounts owed, interpretation of clauses, termination |
| Technology transfer | Assignment contracts, know-how, technical assistance |
| Distribution and franchise contracts | Trademark use, territory, exclusivity clause |
| Trademark coexistence | Coexistence agreements between similar signs |
| Confidentiality and trade secrets | Breach of NDA, misuse of know-how |
| Unfair competition | Misappropriation of trade dress in a contractual context |
| Domain names | .br disputes (SACI-Adm) and gTLDs (UDRP) |
| IP asset value | Price determination, valuation in M&A |

The logic is the same that guides any arbitration: if the right can be the subject of a transaction between the parties, it can be arbitrated. Disputes **between private parties** over IP contracts are, as a rule, fully arbitrable — including matters involving technical analysis of patents, provided the conflict has a contractual origin.

## What CANNOT be arbitrated: validity before the INPI

The most important limit lies in the **validity of the registration** granted by the INPI. The nullity of a patent or trademark is not a disposable right: it involves public interest, and the INPI is a necessary party.

- **Patents:** the [nullity action (Article 56 of the LPI)](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/leis/l9279.htm) may be filed at any time during its term, by the INPI or by any person with legitimate interest, and is brought before the **Federal Court**, with mandatory intervention by the INPI (Article 57).
- **Trademarks:** a registration granted in disagreement with the law is null (Article 165 of the LPI), and nullity may be declared administratively by the INPI itself or judicially.

This does not mean that validity is outside arbitration in all scenarios: in disputes **between private parties**, arbitrators may analyze validity as an **incidental issue** (for example, to decide whether a licensee must pay royalties for a patent that it claims is invalid), provided the decision produces effects only between the parties — never erga omnes, as would occur in judicial nullity ruling. That is exactly the type of discussion that the case of the 5th CSD-ABPI Moot brought to the debate: how far does arbitral jurisdiction go when contract and registration intersect.

## Why arbitrate IP disputes

| Advantage | Practical impact |
|----------|-----------------|
| **Specialization** | Arbitrators with technical experience (chemists, engineers, trademark specialists) understand the subject matter without relying on lengthy expert reports |
| **Confidentiality** | The procedure is confidential — it protects trade secrets, portfolio strategies, and royalty amounts that a public proceeding would expose |
| **Speed** | Deadlines set by the parties and institution; award in months, not years |
| **International enforcement** | Arbitral awards circulate much more easily than court judgments: Brazil acceded to the New York Convention in 2002 ([Decree 4.311/2002](https://www.planalto.gov.br/ccivil_03/decreto/2002/d4311.htm)), and recognition of foreign arbitral awards is done by the STJ |
| **Finality** | No appeal: the arbitral award can only be set aside in exhaustive hypotheses (Article 32 of Law 9.307/96) |

The point of international enforcement deserves emphasis: in disputes between Brazilian and foreign companies (common in technology licensing and global distribution contracts), arbitration under the rules of an international institution is often the only path that provides real enforcement security outside Brazil.

## Institutions: where to arbitrate in Brazil and abroad

### CSD-ABPI — the center specialized in IP

The [ABPI Dispute Resolution Center](https://csd-abpi.org.br/) is Brazil's main ADR hub specialized in intellectual property, with 15 years of operation. Its structure includes:

- **CArb-ABPI** — arbitration chamber with its own rules, list of arbitrators, and published costs
- **CMed-ABPI** — mediation chamber (procedures as of October 2025)
- **CASD-ND** — domain name chamber, which administers the [SACI-Adm](https://csd-abpi.org.br/) system in partnership with NIC.br for ".br" domain disputes
- **CDD-ABPI** — digital law chamber, with digital mediation, accelerated digital arbitration, and expert determination

CSD-ABPI also publishes reference works on the topic, including arbitration specialized in IP and biotechnology, trade secret disputes, and the discussion on **SEP, FRAND, and arbitration** — a hot topic for those working with standard-essential patents.

### Other chambers in Brazil

CAM-CCBC (São Paulo), CAMARB (Belo Horizonte), FGV (Rio/São Paulo), and CBMA (São Paulo) administer arbitrations involving IP within broader business disputes. The choice depends on the profile of the conflict: for disputes predominantly of a commercial contract nature with ancillary IP, a general chamber suffices; for a 100% IP subject matter, CSD-ABPI offers specialization.

### International: WIPO Arbitration and Mediation Center

The [WIPO AMC](https://www.wipo.int/amc/en/) is the international center specialized in IP and technology disputes, headquartered in Geneva. It offers:

- **Mediation** — consensual process assisted by a mediator
- **Arbitration and Expedited Arbitration** — binding and final decision
- **Expert determination** — for specific technical issues, such as asset valuation or royalty determination
- **Domain name disputes** — global leader in administering cases under the UDRP

WIPO clauses are standard in international licensing and technology transfer contracts involving Brazil.

## Step by step: how to structure arbitration in IP contracts

**1. Choose the appropriate ADR model.** Mediation first (preserves the commercial relationship), arbitration for a final decision, or expert determination for an isolated technical issue (e.g., royalty amount).

**2. Draft the arbitration clause with precision.** The clause must indicate: (i) institution and rules (e.g., CSD-ABPI or WIPO); (ii) seat and language of the arbitration; (iii) number of arbitrators and rule for their selection; (iv) governing law. Generic clauses ("disputes shall be resolved by arbitration") give rise to incidents that delay everything.

**3. Define the scope.** Make it clear that the clause covers contractual disputes, including interpretation, termination, and amounts — and, if desired, the incidental analysis of validity between the parties.

**4. Anticipate confidentiality.** The clause must provide for confidentiality of the proceedings, documents, and award, including for arbitrators and experts.

**5. Prepare for enforcement.** For international contracts, prefer a seat in a country that is a signatory to the New York Convention (practically the entire relevant world) and check the need for recognition by the STJ.

## Arbitration with the public sector: ICTs and the Innovation Law

Since Law 13.129/2015, universities, research institutes, and other entities of the public administration may arbitrate conflicts over disposable patrimonial rights. This is relevant for the innovation ecosystem: [technology transfer contracts registered with the INPI]({{< relref "posts/guia-registro-contratos-transferencia-tecnologia-inpi-2026/" >}}), co-ownership agreements, and technological orders with ICTs may provide for arbitration — a real alternative to the slowness of executions against the government in disputes over royalties and ownership of results.

## What to watch in the coming years

1. **SEP and FRAND** — the definition of fair, reasonable, and non-discriminatory rates for standard-essential patents (5G, IoT) is increasingly migrating to specialized arbitrations, as already occurs in Europe and the US
2. **Digital disputes** — e-commerce, domain names, and data are starting to generate conflicts that digital law chambers (such as CDD-ABPI) absorb with expedited procedures
3. **Growth of the Brazilian market** — the maturation of CSD-ABPI, the training of arbitrators through moots, and regulatory predictability should expand the use of arbitration in licensing and trademark coexistence
